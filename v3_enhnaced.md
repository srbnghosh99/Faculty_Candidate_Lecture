# African Coups in the COVID-19 Era

## A Current History with Extended Empirical Analysis

Shrabani Ghosh · SUNY Polytechnic Institute, AIX Center · July 2026

Between August 2020 and November 2022, Africa experienced eleven
military coup attempts across eight countries — the highest concentration
since the early 1990s. Drawing on the Colpus 1.1 dataset (Chin and
Kirkpatrick, 2023) and GWF Autocratic Regimes data (Geddes, Wright,
and Frantz, 2014), this paper provides both a narrative current history of
each of the eleven cases and an empirical discussion of competing
explanatory 
frameworks. 
New 
statistical 
contributions 
include
point-biserial correlations confirming the coup trap (r=−0.397, p=0.008
for inter-coup interval; r=0.319, p=0.035 for prior coup count) and
Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showing that high-history countries reach
50% inter-coup survival at 2.5 years versus 8.5 years for low-history
countries. The eleven cases are best explained by structural preconditions
— coup trap dynamics, personal and military regime histories, Sahelian
insurgency — amplified by COVID-19 fiscal stress and eroded regional
deterrence, with regional contagion playing an amplifying rather than
independent causal role.

Keywords: military coups, Africa, Sahel, COVID-19, coup trap, democratic
backsliding, contagion, GWF, Colpus, Kaplan-Meier

### 1. Introduction

The period from August 2020 to November 2022 produced eleven military

coup attempts across eight African countries — seven of which succeeded

— constituting the highest concentration of military interventions since the

early 1990s and reversing a three-decade trend of declining coup activity

first documented by Clark (2007). The 63.6 percent success rate matches

the 1960s peak and represents the highest figure in six decades.

Chin and Kirkpatrick (2023) provide the foundational current-history

account using the Colpus 1.1 dataset, framing the central question as: why

did coup activity increase sharply in Africa between August 2020 and

November 2022? This paper follows their methodological framework —

case-by-case narrative analysis combined with systematic quantitative

evidence — and extends it with original statistical tests. Section 2 presents

a narrative case study of each of the eleven coup attempts in chronological

order. Section 3 evaluates competing explanatory frameworks: the coup

trap, COVID-19's role, insurgency pressure, democratic backsliding,

ethnic military composition, and regional contagion.

#### 2.1 Mali — August 18, 2020

##### Successful · Anti-Democratic (AD) · Military Coup

Mali's August 2020 coup opened the wave. On August 18, soldiers

detained President Ibrahim Boubacar Keïta and Prime Minister Boubou

Cissé at Keïta's residence in Bamako following gunfire at the Kati military

base (Maclean, 2020a; Paquette, 2020d). Keïta announced his resignation

on national television that night, dissolving the National Assembly (Bell et

al., 2020). The coup was led by Colonel Assimi Goïta's National

Committee for the Salvation of the People (CNSP). Large protests had

The Koulikoro massacre of November 2019 was a focal point for military

imposed sanctions and demanded civilian rule within twelve months

(Akorlie, 2020). Mali had 4 prior coups, last in 2012.

#### 2.2 Niger — March 31, 2021

##### Failed · Anti-Democratic (AD) · Military Coup

Niger's attempted coup on March 31, 2021 — two days before

President-elect Bazoum's inauguration — was repulsed by loyal forces

before it could seize power (Bell et al., 2021; BBC, 2021c). Six military

officers were arrested (Agence France-Presse, 2022g). Bazoum was

inaugurated as scheduled on April 2 in what the government described as

the country's first peaceful democratic transfer of power (BBC, 2021d).

The attempt reflected military frustration over the jihadist insurgency

along Niger's borders with Mali and Burkina Faso but lacked the organized

coalition that made the Malian coup successful. Niger had 7 prior coups,

last in 2010 (APA, 2021).

#### 2.3 Chad — April 20, 2021

##### Successful · Reshuffling (SH) · Military Coup

Chad's April 2021 coup was unique in the wave: it followed the death in

combat of President Idriss Déby Itno, killed on April 19 visiting troops

fighting the rebel group FACT (Walsh and Schmitt, 2021; Walsh, 2021b).

His son Mahamat Idriss Déby dissolved the government and National

Assembly, declaring an 18-month transition (Adamou et al., 2021). Colpus

codes this as reshuffling because the Déby-Zaghawa ruling coalition

remained in power. It is the only case triggered by a leader's death and the

only father-to-son succession in the wave. Chad had 8 prior coups, last in

2006 (Walsh, 2021a).

#### 2.4 Mali — May 24, 2021

##### Successful · Provisional (PR) · Military Coup

Nine months after the first coup, Colonel Goïta arrested transitional

President Bah N'Daw and Prime Minister Moctar Ouane for attempting to

form a government without military consultation (Bell et al., 2021;

Paquette, 2021a). Goïta was sworn in as transitional president on June 7

(Paquette, 2021b). This provisional regime coup illustrates the

self-reinforcing dynamic documented by Elischer and Lawrance (2022):

the transitional arrangement installed after the first coup became the target

of the second. ECOWAS imposed new sanctions. The junta later set a

February 2024 election target (Africanews, 2022c).

#### 2.5 Guinea — September 5, 2021

##### Successful · Regime Change (CH) · Military Coup

Colonel Mamadi Doumbouya's Special Forces unit seized Guinea's

presidential palace on September 5, detaining President Alpha Condé —

who had manipulated the constitution in 2020 for a third term, triggering

months of violent protests (Allegrozzi, 2020; Bah and Paquette, 2021;

BBC, 2021a). Doumbouya's unit was composed largely of Guerze (Kpelle)

soldiers underrepresented in Condé's Malinke-dominated political order —

the clearest ethnic military dynamic in the wave (Harkness, 2016; Diallo et

al., 2021). Condé's executive aggrandizement is the textbook Bermeo

(2016) backsliding mechanism. Guinea had 4 prior coups, last in 2008.

#### 2.6 Sudan — September 21, 2021

##### Failed · Provisional (PR) · Military Coup

A group of military officers attempted to seize power from Sudan's

civilian-military Sovereignty Council on September 21, but the

government announced suppression within hours (Agence France-Presse,

2021; Beaumont, 2021). The attempt was attributed to elements of the

former Bashir regime and condemned by the AU and Western

governments. It directly foreshadowed the successful coup five weeks later

(Benoist, 2021). Sudan had 15 prior coups — the highest in the dataset —

with its last in 2019: the most extreme coup trap case in the wave.

#### 2.7 Sudan — October 25, 2021

##### Successful · Provisional (PR) · Military Coup

General al-Burhan declared a state of emergency on October 25, detained

Prime Minister Hamdok and dissolved the Sovereignty Council (Salih and

Beaumont, 2021). The coup dismantled Sudan's transitional arrangement

— its most credible path toward civilian rule in three decades. Hamdok

was briefly reinstated under a November agreement but resigned in

January 2022 (Dahir, 2022). Sudan subsequently descended into civil war

between SAF and RSF beginning April 2023 — the wave's most severe

humanitarian consequence (Boswell, 2022). Ali, Ben Hammou, and

Powell (2022) document how successive constitutional manipulations led

here. Like Mali's second coup, this illustrates that provisional regimes are

structurally the most coup-vulnerable government type.

#### 2.8 Burkina Faso — January 24, 2022

##### Successful · Anti-Democratic (AD) · Military Coup

Lieutenant-Colonel Paul-Henri Damiba announced military rule on

January 24 following gunfire at barracks in Ouagadougou and Kaya (Al

Jazeera and News Agencies, 2022; Walsh, 2022). The immediate trigger

was the Inata massacre of November 2021, in which forty gendarmes were

killed awaiting a resupply that never arrived (Dwyer, 2022; Engels,

2022a). This crystallized military resentment of President Kaboré's

security management. Burkina Faso had 8 prior coups, last in 2015, at the

epicenter of the Sahelian insurgency — structurally one of the most

coup-prone countries in the wave (Heywood et al., 2022).

#### 2.9 Guinea-Bissau — February 1, 2022

##### Failed · Anti-Democratic (AD) · Military Coup

An armed group attacked the government building in Bissau during a

cabinet meeting on February 1, killing at least eleven people and briefly

trapping President Embaló (Paquette, 2022; BBC, 2022a). Embaló

declared the situation under control the same day, attributing the attack to

drug trafficking networks. ECOWAS subsequently deployed a stabilizing

force (Dabo and Peyton, 2022b). Guinea-Bissau had 7 prior coups, last in

2012. Unlike the Sahelian cases, there was no active insurgency; the

attempt reflects the country's deep institutional fragility and criminal

network threats.

#### 2.10 Burkina Faso — September 30, 2022

##### Successful · Regime Change (CH) · Military Coup

Captain Ibrahim Traoré deposed Damiba on September 30, citing the

latter's failure to contain the insurgency and alleged contacts with Kaboré's

allies (Associated Press, 2022a; Maclean, 2022). Damiba fled to Togo

(Booty, 2022b). This second Burkinabé coup in nine months is the wave's

clearest provisional regime trap case. Coded as regime change because the

ruling coalition shifted, it also demonstrates the 'enemy within' dynamic:

post-coup juntas face the same successor threats from subordinates they

posed to their predecessors (Roessler, 2011). Burkina Faso subsequently

expelled French forces and invited Wagner Group mercenaries

(Africanews, 2022a; Paquette and Noack, 2021).

#### 2.11 Sao Tome and Principe — November 25, 2022

##### Failed · Anti-Democratic (AD) · Military Coup

An armed group seized the military barracks in Sao Tome on November

25, killing four people, before being suppressed (Agence France-Presse,

2022a; Christensen, 2022). Prime Minister Ramos declared the situation

under control by morning (Associated Press, 2022b). Six arrests followed

(Bonny, 2022). Sao Tome is the wave's structural outlier — only 2 prior

coups (last 2003), no insurgency — suggesting regional demonstration

effects contributed even in a weak-precondition context. The failed

outcome is consistent with that weaker structural profile (BBC, 2022c).

### 3. Discussion

This section evaluates competing explanations for the 2020–2022 wave,

following the analytical structure of Chin and Kirkpatrick (2023). Each

subsection addresses a distinct explanatory question with quantitative

evidence and literature engagement.

#### 3.1 Does Prior Coup History Explain the Wave?

The coup trap is the most robustly replicated finding in the quantitative

coup literature. Londregan and Poole (1990), analyzing 121 countries over

1950–82, find that 'the aftereffects of a coup include a heritage of political

instability in the form of an increased likelihood of further coups.' Collier

and Hoeffler (2005) replicate this for Africa specifically, noting that

'Africa does not have more coups because it is Africa, it has more coups

because it is poor' — but that past coups independently elevate future risk

beyond income effects.

Figure 5 presents two original analyses testing the coup trap on the

2020–2022 wave. Point-biserial correlations confirm that prior coup count

(r=0.319, p=0.035) and years since last coup (r=−0.397, p=0.008) are both

statistically significant predictors. The Kaplan-Meier curves show that

countries with more than five prior coups reach 50% inter-coup survival at

2.5 years versus 8.5 years for low-history countries — a 3.4× difference.

Roessler (2011) provides the political mechanism: leaders purge potential

coup-makers, generating outside rebel coalitions that further destabilize

the regime in a self-perpetuating cycle.

#### 3.2 Did COVID-19 Cause the Wave?

Chin (2022) identifies COVID-19 as a proximate trigger through three

channels: fiscal disruption of state revenues, diversion of international

monitoring attention, and governance failures that delegitimized

incumbents. Dunning (2004) provides the theoretical mechanism for the

attentional channel: when donor credibility and external monitoring are

diverted to pandemic response, the external constraint on military

intervention weakens.

However, Faulkner, Johnson, and Singh (2022) and Singh (2022) caution

against treating COVID-19 as a primary cause. The structural

preconditions in every affected country predated the pandemic by years.

Countries exposed to the same shocks but without structural vulnerability

— Ghana, Senegal, Tanzania — did not experience coups. COVID's

contribution was to compress and synchronize governance crises already

developing, not to create them.

#### 3.3 How Did Islamist Insurgencies Shape Coup Risk?

The Sahelian insurgency nexus distinguishes the 2020–2022 wave from

prior African coup clusters. Every Sahelian country in the wave was

engaged in active counterinsurgency against JNIM or ISGS affiliates. The

case narratives document the specific operational failures — Koulikoro

(Mali), Inata (Burkina Faso) — that activated coup coalitions. Aksoy,

Carter, and Wright (2015) provide systematic evidence that terrorism

increases the probability of irregular leader removal.

Post-coup governments have not resolved the insurgencies. Bell and

Sudduth (2017) find that coup governments face the same insurgent threats

as predecessors but with reduced legitimacy. Jihadist territorial control

expanded in Mali and Burkina Faso after their coups (Martin and

Lebovich, 2022) — the opposite of the stated military rationale. The

expulsion of French forces and invitation of Wagner Group mercenaries

has not reversed this deterioration (Paquette and Noack, 2021).

#### 3.4 What Role Did Democratic Backsliding Play?

Bermeo (2016) identifies executive aggrandizement — elected leaders

concentrating power incrementally — as the most dangerous contemporary

democratic backsliding form for coup risk. Three cases fit precisely:

Condé's third-term constitutional manipulation in Guinea, al-Bashir's

thirty-year consolidation in Sudan, and Niger's attempted coup against an

incoming elected president. Figure 7 shows that anti-democratic coups

(AD) remain elevated while reshuffling coups (CH) have declined sharply,

and provisional regime coups (PR) nearly tripled from 1990–2009 to

2010–2022. Lindberg and Clark (2008) show that democratization reduces

coup risk only when institutions are consolidated; early-stage

democratization may increase risk. Wobig (2015) confirms that democracy

clauses in regional agreements reduce coups — making AU/ECOWAS

enforcement erosion directly coup-facilitating (Tansey, 2017, 2018;

Souaré, 2014).

#### 3.5 How Important Are Ethnic Politics and Military Composition?

Harkness (2016, 2018) finds that ethnic manipulation within security

institutions drives coup attempts in decolonization and democratization

contexts, holding even after controlling for economic and institutional

variables. Guinea is the clearest case: Doumbouya's Guerze-majority unit

exploiting Condé's Malinke-dominated order fits Harkness's mechanism

exactly. In most other cases, however, ethnic politics is secondary to

professional military grievances. Mali's coups were driven by insurgency

frustrations; Sudan's reflected the SAF-RSF institutional fracture; Burkina

Faso's coup coalitions were not straightforwardly ethnically defined.

Ethnic composition matters most when deliberately politicized within the

military and when it coincides with operational grievances (Vogt et al.,

2015).

#### 3.6 Did Coups Spread From One Country to Another?

Chin and Kirkpatrick (2023) find 'little evidence of direct coup diffusion;

most evidence is circumstantial.' Miller, Joseph, and Ohl (2018) across 1.2

million model specifications find results that 'clearly undercut coup

contagion,' and Singh (2022) specifically contests the contagion

explanation for this wave. The shared structural conditions provide an

alternative explanation for the temporal clustering that does not require

diffusion.

Nevertheless, 57.9% of African coups in 2021 were preceded by a

same-region coup within 90 days — well above the 45% long-run average.

A sensitivity analysis across time windows (30d: 26.2%; 60d: 44.9%; 90d:

57.9%; 180d: 79.0%) shows the result is window-dependent, directly

supporting Miller et al.'s (2018) methodological caution. The defensible

position is that contagion operated as a signal-amplifier — updating

perceived coup payoffs in already-susceptible countries — not as an

independent cause.

#### 3.7 Synthesis

The cross-case analysis produces a consistent hierarchy of explanatory

weight. The coup trap — statistically confirmed by both correlation tests

— provides the strongest explanation for country selection. COVID-19

explains timing and synchronization. The Sahel insurgency explains

military grievances in the six Sahelian cases. Democratic backsliding

explains civilian government delegitimization in Guinea, Sudan, and

Niger. Ethnic military composition is significant in Guinea but secondary

elsewhere. Contagion amplified but did not independently generate the

wave.

The consequences for African democracy are severe (Elischer and

Lawrance, 2022). Four countries lost electoral governments. Post-coup

juntas extended transition timelines, suppressed civil society, and expelled

international monitors. Sudan descended into catastrophic civil war.

Post-coup security outcomes are worse, not better. And the structural

conditions remain in place — confirmed by coups in Niger and Gabon in

2023. The wave is not a temporary disruption but a sustained feature of the

African political landscape whose resolution requires the institutional

investments that were not made before it began.
